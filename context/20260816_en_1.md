# "Niu Lai" Grossed Over 800,000 Yuan in a Single Day — Don't Take It as a Joke

By now, everyone scrolling through their feeds has probably seen just how absurd the suddenly viral animated film *Niu Lai* ("The Cow Comes") is. The film reportedly cost less than 10,000 yuan to make and had zero promotion. Because its production quality is so crude and so gloriously bizarre, it spread across the internet as an abstract meme — and the worse it looked, the more "refreshingly terrible" it seemed. Many people said they'd "never seen a movie this bad, so they had to go taste it for themselves and unlock a limited-edition achievement." And that's exactly how this "achievement" push shoved the film's single-day box office past 800,000 yuan.

![](../images/blog/20260816/640.jpg)

Since there's no screening near me for now, I can't go to a theater and "appreciate" it myself, but I've still gotten a good taste of *Niu Lai*'s abstract charm from the countless screen-recording posts on Xiaohongshu. Many of those posts mention that audiences in theaters simply can't stop laughing — they may not even know why they're laughing, but once one person starts, everyone else loses control, and the whole theater fills with a cheerful air.

**The flood of screen-recording posts is itself a major reason *Niu Lai* suddenly took off.** Normally, online opinion is sharply critical of people filming the screen at legitimate theatrical releases. But with a movie like *Niu Lai*, no one is going to condemn the screen-recordings posted online — everyone is too busy having fun — and that has become free promotion for the film. Right now, *Niu Lai* is already the daily champion on Maoyan's national "most-wanted" list.

![](../images/blog/20260816/641.png)

Because screenings are currently concentrated in first- and second-tier cities, the box office is mostly coming from big cities. This is different from the usual works that rely on lower-tier markets. Young people in first- and second-tier cities are also the main audience for internet memes and abstract culture — it's just that such elements rarely show up in theaters. Judging by the current growth, if the film isn't forcibly pulled from theaters, its final box office could be quite substantial.

It's also amusing: ever since film professionals came up with the "movie industry winter" narrative, they've kept getting slapped in the face by one work after another — proof that it isn't the market that's frozen, but their own incompetence. **If even a work like *Niu Lai* can slap them in the face at the box office, some professionals might as well go find a piece of tofu and knock themselves out.** There's something insiders all know but the general public has little idea about: if you genuinely have the ability to make a film, getting a release permit (龙标) for a non-controversial subject isn't that hard — as long as you're willing to lose money to buy attention, you can even get it into theaters.

![](../images/blog/20260816/642.png)

![](../images/blog/20260816/643.png)

**Ever since the domestic AI video boom at the start of the year, I've been waiting for the day when an AI-made cult film shows up in theaters.** Cult films are an important force filling box offices in the Western film market, but they've always been a blank space on the mainland. Traditional cult films often deal with underground subjects and sensory stimulation, which face censorship and audience problems in China. But now, with the development of internet culture and technology, the foundation for "above-ground" cult films to appear in large numbers domestically has already been laid.

In fact, the tolerance for bloody scenes in domestically released films has already expanded noticeably in the past two years. Films like *Backrooms* and *痴迷* (Chi Mi), which are also cult-style and took off in the U.S. this year, have achieved respectable box office results in China, and the blood-soaked splatter classic *Kill Bill* is about to be re-released too. Meanwhile, the thriving development of all kinds of internet subcultures and abstract culture has opened up an enormous space of subject matter for cult films. Backrooms, dreamcore, fake humans (伪人), and other cultural currents are extremely popular on Chinese networks as well, so it's easy to imagine localized *Backrooms* and *Chi Mi* appearing.

And then, most importantly, there's the rise of AI video technology. So far, AI video tools have proven to be more vital in the hands of ordinary netizens than in the hands of professionals. The AI videos made by regular people may be crude, but they win through total creative freedom: abstract, surreal, with unlimited imagination and inventiveness. **Stack these on top of all the emerging, imaginative subcultural subjects, and you get an inexhaustible production base for a new kind of above-ground cult cinema — arguably an inevitable path of "creative technology democratization" in the AI era.**

![](../images/blog/20260816/644.png)

The reason *Backrooms* and *Chi Mi* could explode is first of all that their related elements were already hugely popular on short-video platforms. For young American audiences, a novel, trendy hook plus short-video buzz is the first reason they walked into theaters to champion these two films.

*Kung Fu Women's Football*, which was hot in theaters recently, also adopted a short-video-style narrative, but its elements have nothing to do with novelty or trendiness. Hong Kong filmmakers once deeply understood the essence of cult films — it's a pity that we can hardly see any of it in the Hong Kong filmmakers who remain today. Let me give an example: **if we set the question from the angles of novelty, AI, cult, and subculture, and asked Hong Kong filmmakers — asked Stephen Chow — to make a movie right now, what could they make?**

Readers can probably guess: the "Snow Mountain Rescues a Fox, Jiangban Duck Repays the Kindness" series of AI short videos, built on the foundation of Shaw Brothers action cinema. It was precisely the wave of fan re-creations of the Jiangban Duck series that drove the first large-scale popularization of AI video on Chinese networks. This subject matter is both very close to young people's lives and full of reflection on new AI technology, and its slapstick, endless-twist style fits Stephen Chow and the creative temperament of Hong Kong cinema's golden age perfectly. Imagine if Hong Kong filmmakers could keenly grasp the pulse of the times and expand the Jiangban Duck meme into a full movie — fusing reflections on new technology with the comedic spirit and nostalgia of Hong Kong cinema's glory days. Even if it were delivered at only seventy or eighty percent of the old power, wouldn't that be a perfect farewell — or even a rebirth — for the golden age of Hong Kong cinema?

![](../images/blog/20260816/645.png)

Another AI creation trend that took off after the Jiangban Duck series is "Hua Qiang Buys a Melon." The recently released *Johnny Keep Walking! 2* used a time-loop setup, but the result wasn't great. Yet the Hua Qiang Buys a Melon meme, developed through all kinds of fan re-creations, already contains all the essential elements of a classic time-loop movie. Setting copyright issues aside for now, if someone actually wanted to make this film, then beyond designing a good script, all they'd need to do is add some deliberately AI-abstract elements.

These are just two examples, **illustrating that cultural and technological development has already advanced enough to support ordinary people in producing cult films that can reach the big screen. It's just that no one expected the first film to make the public realize ordinary people can enter theaters with low-cost production would be *Niu Lai*, made with traditional methods.** Keep in mind that today's AI can easily generate image quality and smoothness that completely dwarfs *Niu Lai*.

![](../images/blog/20260816/646.jpg)

In an age flooded with AI, the "retro" look of *Niu Lai* has actually helped people quickly escape the "refined homogenization" that AI visuals bring, dreaming back to the era of crude 3D animation. It's like being fed up with fried food and suddenly getting a bite of fish mint — your mouth instantly feels refreshed. Perhaps in this era of exploding polished visuals, many people need to relearn how to "imagine bad movies."

Of course, I'm not saying we should encourage making bad films. Viewers with more than a decade of theater-going experience all came through the era of bad movies, and nobody wants to go back. But even if *Niu Lai* is bad, it's fundamentally different from the bad movies piled up by entertainment capital, celebrity stars, and traditional filmmakers. **In terms of production cost and mechanism, it genuinely belongs to ordinary people; in terms of expression, with a bit of refinement using today's technology and ideas, it could transform from a purely abstract, bizarre, crude work into a low-cost, grassroots work that fits the cultural trends of the era and is worth a look.**

![](../images/blog/20260816/647.jpg)

So I hope that when the media and Chinese filmmakers discuss the explosion of *Niu Lai*, **they won't casually and arrogantly slap labels like "ugly-appreciation" or "bizarre fetish" on it, but will truly see from this phenomenon the enormous potential of the domestic film market — and really think about how to attract audiences, especially the new generation and the lower-tier markets, into theaters while ensuring quality.**

![](../images/blog/20260816/648.png)

![](../images/blog/20260816/649.png)

This, of course, is not something the film industry can accomplish alone. Reasonable ticket prices, sufficient leisure time, and a good social atmosphere are all important conditions for making people willing to go to the movies. But if filmmakers themselves don't make an effort to deliver good works, everything else is water without a source.

This time, *Niu Lai*'s "going viral" first in first- and second-tier cities also shows that, in many cases, there isn't really a clear line between first- and second-tier cities and lower-tier markets. For a short while, *Niu Lai* even became an "exclusive" for big-city audiences. Could this make those who cooked up the marketing line about "advance screenings of *The Odyssey* as the newest ID card of the middle class" feel awkward?

![](../images/blog/20260816/650.png)

Netizens have made crossover posters of *Niu Lai* x *The Odyssey*. In one, the enemies Odysseus faces have all become Niu Lai; in another, Odysseus's companions have become "Niu De-sai" (Niu + Odyssey), "Niu De-xiu-si" (Niu + Odysseus), "Niu Jia-men-nong" (Niu + Agamemnon), Apollo's cows... This is clearly another product of the collision between internet abstract culture and AI-driven technological democratization.

Because of audience demographics and IMAX theater distribution, *The Odyssey*'s box office is also concentrated in first- and second-tier cities, with predictions around 700 million yuan. *Niu Lai* naturally can't pose any threat to *The Odyssey*, but in my view, the "heat" of *Niu Lai* has genuinely added an important piece to my confidence in the future of Chinese cinema.

![](../images/blog/20260816/651.png)

![](../images/blog/20260816/652.jpg)

There really is Apollo's cow in "Niu De-sai." In terms of market, we're the largest; in terms of film industry technology, we can already go toe-to-toe with Hollywood in quite a few areas; in terms of the needs of the era, the future mission of Chinese cinema isn't just to serve domestic audiences but also the Global South. Now *Niu Lai* has reminded everyone in China with imagination, talent, and drive: in this era of technological democratization, as long as you can truly make something worth seeing, people won't just see it in cyberspace — they'll see it in the real world too. This is the cultural bugle call of the era of technological democratization, and the death knell for those old-guard film insiders who can't reach the heights and refuse to settle for the low.

The spiral of the times is often exactly like this.
