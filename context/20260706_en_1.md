# Ensemble Virtual Metrology Models of Semiconductor Processes: Using LASSO + RF to Bring Semiconductor Metrology to “Real-Time Prediction”

![](../images/blog/半导体VM概念封面图.png)

> This paper was published in *IEEE Sensors Journal* on May 1, 2026, under the title *Ensemble Virtual Metrology Models of Semiconductor Processes*, by authors Yawei Chen, Shaohua Chang, and Eric Chien from the Yield Enhancement Team of the 8A Fab Manufacturing Center at China Resources Microelectronics.

The object of study in this paper is not a generic machine-learning toy problem, but a very specific and deeply engineering-oriented problem in semiconductor manufacturing: during the BEOL stage of 0.13 μm wafer fabrication, when depositing FSG dielectric films using high-density plasma (HDP) processes, how can equipment sensor data be used to predict the fluorine concentration in the film in real time? The paper’s main model, M4, is a two‑level feature‑extraction ensemble virtual metrology model. Its core combination is correlation‑enhanced feature screening, second‑order polynomial features, LASSO sparse selection, and Random Forest nonlinear modeling. Experimental results show that M4 achieves R² = 0.985, MAE = 0.011, RMSE = 0.009, and MAPE = 0.173 on 4,632 wafers, with training time, prediction time, and interpretability all kept within a range feasible for engineering deployment.

If we translate this paper into a beginner‑friendly perspective on semiconductors, the real problem it aims to solve can be stated in one sentence: a fab cannot physically measure every critical process outcome on every wafer, because metrology tools are slow, expensive, create queues, and consume cycle time. But if we can learn a mapping from process parameters to quality metrics from the equipment’s FDC sensor data, we can deliver near‑real‑time quality predictions before the wafer even reaches a physical metrology tool. This is *virtual metrology* (VM). It does not necessarily completely replace physical metrology, but it can turn sample‑based offline inspection into higher‑frequency, more wafer‑by‑wafer online quality monitoring.

*Derivative knowledge note: In semiconductor manufacturing, metrology refers to the measurement of critical dimensions, film thickness, concentration, overlay, etch depth, and other quality indicators. Traditional physical metrology typically relies on dedicated tools such as ellipsometers, CD‑SEMs, film‑thickness gauges, and optical metrology systems. Virtual metrology uses the sensor traces, recipe parameters, and historical metrology results recorded by the process equipment itself to train a model that predicts the results that would otherwise be produced by the metrology tools. Its value is not just “saving one metrology step,” but more importantly shortening the feedback loop: when process drift, chamber condition changes, or an abnormal lot occurs, the model can provide early signals to APC, SPC, MES, and other systems.*

## I. Why Does Semiconductor Manufacturing Need VM? Not “One More Model,” but “One Less Wait”

The paper’s introduction starts from the real‑world pressures faced by fabs: with AI and advanced manufacturing, equipment parameters are increasing, sensors are becoming denser, and couplings between processes are growing stronger. Traditional statistical‑sampling‑based physical metrology cannot cover every slot of every lot, let alone perform complete inspection on every critical layer of every wafer. For yield‑enhancement teams, this creates a classic contradiction: on one hand, they want to detect problems earlier, expand sampling coverage, and improve chamber matching; on the other hand, they cannot infinitely increase the number of physical metrology tools, metrology time, and engineering hold time.

This is where VM matters. It uses FDC data, recipe parameters, and historical physical‑metrology data from process equipment to train a predictive model. Immediately after a process run, the model outputs quality predictions and confidence indicators, and the APC system uses these results for dynamic compensation or anomaly interception. The paper explicitly states that VM can reduce tool downtime, calibration, test runs, and rework, and can also suppress periodic fluctuations caused by abnormalities. These may sound like manufacturing‑management language, but they all trace back to the same underlying principle: the sooner you know the quality status of a wafer, the less you let problems propagate downstream.

*Derivative knowledge note: FDC stands for Fault Detection and Classification. It collects data such as pressure, temperature, gas flow, power, current, voltage, valve angles, and process time during equipment operation. APC is Advanced Process Control, which adjusts processes based on measurement results or model predictions. SPC is Statistical Process Control, which monitors process stability via control charts, specification limits, and similar methods. MES is the Manufacturing Execution System, which handles lots, recipes, equipment status, holds/releases, and other production execution actions. When VM is truly deployed, it is typically not an isolated model but must be embedded into the closed loops of these systems.*

The paper also uses patent trends to illustrate the industrial heat around VM. The authors searched IPC class G01 (measurement‑related categories) with keywords “virtual metrology” and “semiconductor manufacturing,” showing that global VM‑related patent publications rose significantly after 2004. By process category, CVD, PVD, and etch account for the highest shares, together about 59%; CMP and lithography account for about 14% and 9%, respectively. This also aligns with intuition: deposition and etching equipment have rich process‑sensor data, and their process outcomes depend heavily on chamber state, gas flows, power, and time, making them very suitable for VM modeling.

## II. What Exactly Does This Paper Predict? Fluorine Concentration in HDP FSG Films

The target variable of this paper is the fluorine concentration in FSG dielectric films. FSG is fluorosilicate glass, a type of low‑k dielectric material that may be used in the BEOL interconnect stage. BEOL (Back End of Line) refers to the back‑end processes after transistors are fabricated, including metal interconnects, dielectric deposition, vias/trenches, etc. In the paper’s scenario, the FSG film is deposited by an HDP process. HDP stands for high‑density plasma. HDP CVD is characterized by reactive gases excited in a plasma, combining deposition with a certain degree of sputtering/fill capability, and is often used for films that require high gap‑fill quality.

Figure 4 in the paper shows the main framework of the HDP equipment, key chamber components, and the FSG process application. The equipment includes wafer load locks, transfer robots, process chambers, cooling chambers, etc. Inside the process chamber are a shower head, MFCs for reactive gas control, an FDC system, a temperature control system, a bias power system, and a pump system. The reaction scheme given is `SiH4 + SiF4 + O2 -> FSG + byproducts`. Therefore, from a material‑formation perspective, the fluorine concentration in FSG will inevitably be affected by the fluorine‑containing gas SiF4, oxygen O2, silane SiH4, plasma power, chamber temperature, pressure, process time, and other variables. However, this influence is not purely linear: gas supply, plasma energy, surface adsorption, and chamber state are all mutually coupled.

*Derivative knowledge note: The lower the dielectric constant, the smaller the capacitance between interconnect lines, and the lower the RC delay, which benefits both chip speed and power consumption. FSG lowers the dielectric constant by introducing fluorine into the SiO₂ network, but the fluorine content also affects film stability, stress, moisture absorption, and subsequent integration risks. Therefore, fluorine concentration is not a “the more the better” metric; it needs to stay stably within the process window. For a fab, if this indicator can only be obtained via offline sample metrology, blind spots between batches are inevitable.*

[Illustration suggestion 3: Directly capture Figure 4 from the paper] Caption: “HDP equipment and FSG process schematic. MFCs control reactive gas inputs, RF power maintains the plasma environment, FDC collects chamber state and process parameters, and the final fluorine concentration is determined by a combination of multiple parameters.”

Table I in the paper selects 18 top FDC sensors/process features, including Ar flow, O2 flow, SiF4 flow, SiH4 side flow, SiH4 top flow, chamber inter/outer He flow, chamber pressure, electrostatic chuck current/voltage, Bias RF forward, Side RF forward, Top RF forward, He leak rate, process time, throttle valve angle, dome heater temperature, and chamber temperature. These variables cover gas supply, pressure/pumping, temperature, plasma energy, ESC status, and process duration, basically corresponding to the major control quantities that determine reaction rate, film composition, and within‑wafer uniformity in a CVD/HDP process.

*Derivative knowledge note: MFC is a mass flow controller, with units commonly in SCCM. ESC is the electrostatic chuck, used to hold the wafer and also affecting thermal contact and wafer temperature. RF forward power is the forward radio‑frequency power, reflecting the energy delivered to the plasma system. The throttle valve angle affects pumping and chamber pressure. Dome heater/chamber temperatures influence reaction kinetics and surface processes. These variables themselves are not equivalent to the film’s fluorine concentration, but they constitute the “evidence chain of what happened during the process.”*

## III. Core Algorithm: Two‑Level Feature Extraction Is Not Just Stacking Models — It’s “Denoising Then Fitting” for a Complex Process

The modeling framework proposed in the paper can be summarized as “enhanced feature screening + polynomial LASSO + RF.” The authors call it a two‑level feature extraction ensemble machine learning model. The motivation is clear: linear models like LASSO are lightweight, interpretable, and capable of feature selection, but they tend to underfit when faced with strongly coupled, nonlinear advanced‑process parameters. Random Forest, as a nonlinear ensemble model, can capture complex patterns and is relatively robust, but with redundant features, small samples, high‑dimensional interactions, and hyperparameter tuning, it can easily encounter problems of complexity, overfitting, and interpretability. What the paper does is not simply concatenate two models; it uses LASSO for sparsification and linear/polynomial interaction extraction, then lets RF handle nonlinear fitting.

In more plain terms, the first level asks: “Among these sensor variables and their second‑order interaction terms, which ones are truly relevant to the target?” The second level then asks: “Among the retained key signals and original variables, are there more complex nonlinear combinations that can explain the fluorine concentration?” The benefit is that RF does not need to blindly split among a large pool of redundant features, and LASSO does not have to bear all nonlinear relationships alone. The paper encapsulates this process in Algorithm 1: input feature group ALT, target variable VAR, correlation threshold λ=0.1, polynomial order d=2, tree count N=100; first compute correlations between features and target, retain features with absolute correlation above threshold, then perform LASSO on polynomial features, then feed the first‑level outputs and nonlinear features to an RF regressor, finally compute R² and interpret with SHAP.

![](../images/blog/半导体虚拟量测论文机制图.png)

*Derivative knowledge note: LASSO stands for least absolute shrinkage and selection operator; it adds an L1 regularization term to the linear‑regression loss function. The L1 penalty forces some coefficients to zero, thus naturally performing feature selection. Random Forest is a tree‑model ensemble under the bagging idea, where each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample and a random subset of features, and regression results are averaged. RF’s strengths are nonlinearity, interactions, and robustness; its weaknesses are that with too many noisy input features, model complexity and interpretation costs rise.*

The polynomial feature transformation in the paper can be understood as adding interaction terms to the original variables. With original features \(X=\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\}\) and polynomial order \(d=2\), the model considers not only individual variables but also combined effects like `temperature * pressure`, `SiF4 flow * RF power`, etc. This is very reasonable in semiconductor processes, because many results are not determined by a single knob. For example, SiF4 flow provides the fluorine source, but the final incorporation of fluorine into the film also depends on plasma energy, chamber temperature, surface reaction environment, and process time. The problem with polynomial expansion is dimensional explosion, so the paper immediately uses LASSO to compress useless terms.

## IV. Enhanced Feature Extraction: The Heatmap Is Not the Answer, but the First Sieve

Figure 5 in the paper uses a correlation heatmap to show the Pearson correlation coefficients between the 18 process parameters and the physical metrology values. Based on this, the authors identify six key parameters: dome heater temperature, electrostatic chuck voltage, O2 flow, SiF4 flow, side forward power, and process time. Notably, the correlation coefficients in the heatmap are not dramatic; the values relative to physical metrology values are roughly in the 0.17–0.25 range—for example, dome heater temperature around 0.25, SiF4 flow around 0.24, process time around 0.23, O2 flow around 0.22, side RF forward around 0.19, and ESC voltage around 0.17. This indicates that the linear explanatory power of any single variable for the target is limited.

This is precisely the key to why the paper’s method works. If a single feature had an extremely strong linear relationship with fluorine concentration, a simple linear model might suffice; but in real processes, the correlation of each individual variable is not high, suggesting that the target is influenced by multi‑variable combinations, nonlinear relationships, and process states. Enhanced feature extraction first uses correlation as an initial sieve to down‑weight irrelevant or overly noisy variables, then captures more valuable interactions through second‑order polynomials and LASSO, and finally hands things over to RF to learn nonlinear boundaries. This is a very engineering‑oriented compromise: it neither blindly trusts a black‑box model nor fantasizes that linear correlations can explain the entire process.

*Derivative knowledge note: The Pearson correlation coefficient only measures linear association, not causality, and it is not good at discovering threshold effects, saturation effects, or multi‑variable interactions. For example, a certain gas flow may be positively correlated with fluorine concentration within the normal range, but beyond a certain window it may cause non‑uniform reactions, leading to larger prediction errors. Semiconductor processes often exhibit this “locally linear + globally nonlinear” pattern, so correlation heatmaps are suitable for exploration and screening but not for final interpretation.*

## V. Ablation Experiments Tell Us: M4 Wins Because of the Order of Modules

The most convincing part of the paper is the ablation comparison in Table IV. The authors set up five models: M1 is the conventional LASSO+RF baseline; M2 removes the first‑level polynomial‑LASSO linear feature extraction; M3 removes enhanced FE; M4 is the complete two‑level polynomial‑LASSO‑RF ensemble; M5 removes the second‑level RF nonlinear feature extraction. The results show that M4 achieves R²=0.985, MAE=0.011, RMSE=0.009, and MAPE=0.173, the best among all five. M1 already achieves R²=0.964 and RMSE=0.026, which is not bad, but M4 still improves markedly. M2 drops to R²=0.942, indicating that without the first‑level polynomial LASSO, a lot of interaction information is lost. M3 gives R²=0.965 and RMSE=0.021, showing that enhanced FE contributes to noise reduction and retention of strong features. M5 gives R²=0.939 and RMSE=0.038, indicating that without the second‑level RF, nonlinear fitting capability is insufficient.

The value of this set of experiments is that it does not merely say “my model is better than baseline,” but breaks down *why* it is better. The complete model does not win because the algorithm name is more complex; it wins because the order is rational: first use correlation to filter out obviously weak signals, then use LASSO to perform sparse selection in polynomial space, and finally use RF to absorb nonlinear relationships. In other words, M4 both reduces RF’s ineffective search space and provides LASSO with the sparse linear/interaction features it is good at handling. For industrial data, this kind of ordering is often more important than blindly stacking larger models.

| Model | Structural meaning | R² | MAE | RMSE | MAPE |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| M1 | Conventional LASSO+RF baseline | 0.964 | 0.021 | 0.026 | 0.294 |
| M2 | Remove first‑level FE | 0.942 | 0.032 | 0.041 | 0.404 |
| M3 | Remove enhanced FE | 0.965 | 0.025 | 0.021 | 0.305 |
| M4 | Complete model | 0.985 | 0.011 | 0.009 | 0.173 |
| M5 | Remove second‑level FE | 0.939 | 0.030 | 0.038 | 0.395 |

*Derivative knowledge note: R² measures the proportion of variance in the target variable explained by the model; closer to 1 is better. MAE is the mean absolute error. RMSE is the root‑mean‑square error, which is more sensitive to large errors. MAPE is the mean absolute percentage error. Industrial scenarios do not look at a single metric, because high R² may mask local anomalies, and large errors may have disproportionately large impacts on yield. In VM, RMSE and outlier performance are especially important, because model outputs may trigger lot holds, recipe adjustments, or engineering actions.*

## VI. Cross‑Validation and Stress Testing: The Model Must Not Only Be Accurate, but Also Robust to Dirty Data

The paper uses data from 4,632 wafers, split 70%/20%/10% into training, validation, and test sets — i.e., 3,243 for training, 926 for validation, and 463 for test. The authors also perform five‑fold cross‑validation on M4. Table II shows the five‑fold RMSE distributions across the three data splits; the paper text states that the 95% CI RMSE ranges from 0.0878 to 0.0943, and Figure 7 also shows bar charts of RMSE for each fold. One needs to read this carefully: Table IV reports M4’s overall RMSE as 0.009, while the cross‑validation RMSE in Table II/Fig. 7 is around 0.09. There is a scale inconsistency that may relate to unit scaling, a `×10^-3` notation in the table header, the dimensionality of the target variable, or typesetting/text description discrepancies in the paper. In a technical review, it is appropriate to faithfully note this phenomenon rather than forcibly conflating the two sets of numbers.

Even more relevant to the production floor than cross‑validation is stress testing. The paper sets up four types of perturbations: concept drift (3% sudden data injection), missing values (20% simulated missing data), noise injection (Gaussian noise with 10% standard deviation), and outliers (10% extreme outliers). The results show that under these four perturbations, M4’s R² values are 0.977, 0.962, 0.933, and 0.941, corresponding to degradations of about 0.008, 0.023, 0.052, and 0.044. In comparison, conventional M1, with a baseline R²=0.964, drops to 0.902, 0.927, 0.895, and 0.889 under the same perturbations, being especially more sensitive to noise injection and outliers. The authors thus conclude that M4’s degradation can be kept below 0.055, showing better robustness than conventional M1.

*Derivative knowledge note: Concept drift means that the data distribution changes over time; in semiconductors, this may come from chamber aging, post‑PM state changes, gas purity fluctuations, part replacements, recipe fine‑tuning, or environmental variations. Missing values often arise from sensor acquisition anomalies, communication packet loss, or data‑system synchronization issues. Noise and outliers may come from sensor drift, transient disturbances, valve/pressure fluctuations, etc. Industrial models are most afraid of “looking great on the test set but collapsing online with dirty data,” so stress testing is a very valuable part of VM papers.*

## VII. Horizontal Benchmarking and Computational Efficiency: The Engineering Flavor of M4 Lies in CPU Feasibility and Sufficiently Fast Prediction

The paper also compares M4 against LASSO, RF, XGBoost, RF+XGBoost, and conventional M1. In Table VI, LASSO achieves only R²=0.256 with RMSE=0.113, showing that a pure linear sparse model cannot handle the complexity of this scenario. RF achieves R²=0.942 and RMSE=0.032, already demonstrating the advantage of tree models for nonlinearity. XGBoost gives R²=0.824 and RMSE=0.047; RF+XGBoost gives R²=0.901 and RMSE=0.054; conventional M1 gives R²=0.964 and RMSE=0.026; and M4 finally reaches R²=0.985 and RMSE=0.009. The scatter plots in Figure 9 also visually show that M4’s points are closest to the diagonal.

On the efficiency side, the paper implements the model on an Intel Core i7‑13700 CPU with 16 cores, 24 threads, Python 3.11/PyCharm, and evaluates complexity using GOPS, parameter count, training time, and single‑point prediction time. M4 has GOPS=0.31, about 0.7M parameters, training time 247 s, and prediction time 0.089 ms. In comparison, M1 has GOPS=0.37, training time 468 s, and prediction time 0.065 ms; RF+XGBoost has GOPS=0.41, training time 1655 s, and prediction time 0.191 ms. M4’s single‑point prediction time is slightly slower than M1 and RF, but still far below the ~200 ms level required for online operation, and its training time is significantly better than M1 and RF+XGBoost.

This part is important because models in a fab are not Kaggle leaderboard entries. A VM model must run within the existing IT/OT environment, preferably without heavy reliance on GPUs; training iterations must be fast; single‑point prediction latency must be low; and outputs must be consumable by APC/MES/SPC. The paper emphasizes that M4 can be trained on CPU and could even be deployed on FPGA or MCU platforms in the future, indicating that the authors’ goal is not to prove how powerful a deep model can be, but to keep algorithmic complexity within the limits that industrial systems can tolerate.

*Derivative knowledge note: GOPS stands for giga operations per second, a rough measure of computational complexity. Single‑point prediction time is especially critical for VM, because FDC data may continuously enter the system per wafer, per lot, per step, or per time window; if prediction latency is too high, APC cannot perform timely interception or compensation. Training time affects model iteration: process development, post‑PM recovery, chamber matching, and recipe setup may all require rapid retraining or recalibration of the model.*

## VIII. SHAP Interpretation: From “The Model Says So” to “Which Process Variables Are Speaking”

The problem with ensemble models is often lack of interpretability. The paper introduces SHAP to explain the feature contributions of M4. The idea of SHAP comes from Shapley values: treat a single prediction as the result of multiple features “cooperating,” then estimate the marginal contribution of each feature to the prediction. Figure 10 in the paper presents a SHAP summary plot and a ranking by mean absolute SHAP values. The most important feature is Bias RF forward, with a mean SHAP of about 0.0396; followed by chamber outer He flow at about 0.0196; SiH4 top flow at about 0.0172; SiF4 flow at about 0.0165; dome heater temperature at about 0.0122; O2 flow at about 0.0038; SiH4 side flow at about 0.0030; chamber inter He flow at about 0.0024; and ESC current near 0.

This ranking is broadly consistent with process knowledge. Bias RF forward represents the bias‑related energy of the plasma with respect to the wafer, which has a major impact on ion bombardment, surface reactions, and film formation. SiF4 and SiH4 are key reactive gases for FSG formation, determining reactant supply. O2 participates in the oxidation environment and film‑network formation. Dome heater temperature affects reaction kinetics. He flows may be related to backside cooling, heat conduction, chamber environment, or equipment state. The paper notes that the core gas‑phase reaction parameters such as power and reaction‑gas flow rates have the highest SHAP values, consistent with the CVD mechanistic understanding that “reactive‑gas concentrations determine reactant supply.”

However, it should also be noted that SHAP is a post‑hoc attribution, i.e., an after‑the‑fact explanation. It can tell us which features the model “relies on” under the data distribution, but it is not equivalent to strict causal proof. For example, a He flow feature might be important because it directly affects temperature, or it might merely be a proxy for some chamber state. A power variable with high contribution might also be coupled with recipe steps, chamber load, or other parameters. The paper also acknowledges in its limitations that SHAP does not directly incorporate CVD physics, such as gas‑phase reactions and surface adsorption, so its causal‑analysis capability for strongly coupled parameters is limited.

*Derivative knowledge note: In semiconductor manufacturing, interpretability has two layers of value. The first is engineering trust: when process engineers see top features aligned with mechanism, they become more willing to use the model. The second is problem diagnosis: when a batch of wafers shows unusually high or low VM predictions, SHAP can indicate whether power, gas, temperature, or pressure‑related variables are driving the prediction, thus shortening the troubleshooting path. However, SHAP cannot replace DOE, mechanistic models, or engineering verification.*

## IX. From Model to Fab System: The Real Endpoint of VM Is Closed‑Loop Control

Figure 11 in the paper compares a conventional system with an enhanced APC system. The conventional approach relies on FDC, MES, and SPC operating separately, with quality feedback provided via physical‑metrology sampling. The enhanced scheme integrates an AI‑driven APC system with FDC/MES/SPC, collecting process‑cluster sensor data, combining it with physical‑metrology data from SPC to train and validate the model, and using MES to block abnormal equipment or abnormal lots. System outputs include automatic prediction results, dynamic thresholds, VM data, and interpretability reports. The authors also mention that AI‑driven kernel system commands follow TCP/IP protocols, supporting both inline and cloud deployment.

This indicates that the VM in this paper is not an isolated notebook, but a component of a production system. FDC is responsible for “seeing what happened on the equipment,” SPC for “confirming what the actual quality results are,” MES for “deciding production actions,” and APC for “adjusting the process based on results.” The VM model plays the role of a translator in the middle: converting massive equipment process data into quality predictions, then handing the predictions to the manufacturing system for actions. If this closed loop runs stably, it can improve metrology coverage and anomaly response speed without increasing physical‑metrology hardware.

*Derivative knowledge note: In a fab, bottlenecks are not always production tools; sometimes metrology tools are also bottlenecks. Metrology tools are non‑productive equipment — they do not directly process wafers, but they determine whether a lot can be released to the next step. If a critical layer must wait for metrology results, WIP queues build up. By reducing unnecessary physical metrology, providing early warnings of anomalies, and assisting dynamic sampling, VM can simultaneously improve cycle time, WIP flow, and metrology‑tool capacity.*

## X. Limitations and Next Steps: Cross‑Chamber, Long‑Term Drift, and Mechanism Integration

The paper is quite candid in its limitations section. First, the training data come from a single type of CVD tool (Applied Materials equipment) and a limited batch, so cross‑chamber generalization remains constrained. Second, the model does not adequately handle distribution drift caused by chamber gas‑purity variations. Third, offline training lacks adaptive updating, so long‑term stability against chamber degradation, temporal drift, and post‑PM state changes is insufficient. Fourth, SHAP interpretation remains post‑hoc attribution, without explicit integration of CVD physics such as gas‑phase reactions and surface adsorption. Fifth, although the model is discussed within an APC closed‑loop context, the dry‑run environment does not fully cover real‑world disturbances such as flow fluctuations, pressure ripple, gas interruptions, etc.

These limitations are very real and point to the difficulties of industrial VM. Semiconductor equipment is not a static testbench; the same recipe on different chambers, different PM cycles, different consumable lifetimes, and different gas states can produce distribution differences. A model trained only on historical data, without online updating or domain adaptation, can easily drift out of accuracy over months. Therefore, after the conclusion, the authors propose future directions: develop incremental learning and domain adaptation to dynamically accommodate chamber degradation, post‑maintenance drift, and cross‑tool distribution shifts, improving long‑term temporal stability.

*Derivative knowledge note: Incremental learning means the model continuously absorbs new data for updates, rather than retraining from scratch each time. Domain adaptation handles the problem where the training‑data distribution and the target‑deployment distribution differ. For a fab, these are critical because chamber matching and tool‑to‑tool variation always exist. If VM is to move from a single‑point demo to large‑scale deployment, it must solve the questions: “Can it still be accurate when switching chambers? After PM? After six months?”*

## XI. My Understanding of This Paper: Its Contribution Is Not “Inventing LASSO or RF,” but Placing Them into Fab Language

From a machine‑learning perspective, LASSO, Random Forest, polynomial features, and SHAP are not new algorithms. But the value of this paper lies in organizing them into an engineering structure suitable for semiconductor VM, and discussing cycle time, measurement capacity, wafer yield, APC, FDC/MES/SPC integration, and deployable complexity around real fab metrics. For an industrial paper, this combination of “algorithm + process object + system closed loop + KPIs” is far more meaningful than merely pursuing algorithmic novelty.

In my view, the three most valuable lessons from this paper are as follows. First, it does not blindly worship complex models; rather, it acknowledges that linear and nonlinear models each have boundaries, and uses two‑level FE to let them complement each other. Second, it carries ablation experiments down to the module level, proving the respective contributions of enhanced FE, polynomial‑LASSO, and RF. Third, it does not treat SHAP as decorative graphics, but attempts to align feature importance with CVD process mechanisms, making the model interpretation understandable to engineers. Of course, the paper also leaves questions worth further pursuit: the scale discrepancy between cross‑validation RMSE and overall RMSE needs additional verification; whether the high accuracy on single‑tool data can transfer to more chambers; and whether real online closed‑loop interventions introduce new feedback biases.

If this paper is used as introductory material for semiconductor AI, its greatest inspiration to me is this: virtual metrology is not simply “take sensor data and train a regression model.” Rather, it must answer four sequential questions. First, where is physical metrology slow, and which waiting segment is VM intended to replace or supplement? Second, what do process mechanisms tell us about which sensor variables are likely meaningful? Third, how can the model maintain accuracy, robustness, and interpretability under high dimensionality, strong coupling, small samples, and drift? Fourth, how do predictions enter the engineering closed loop of FDC/MES/SPC/APC to truly change cycle time, capacity, and yield? Only when all four questions are properly answered can VM move from paper metrics to fab value.
