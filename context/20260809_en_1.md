# Paper Deep-Dive: Navigating Defect Detection with Metrology Data — Samsung × KLA's MDS Platform in Practice

> **Original Paper**: *Enhancing defect detection and yield prediction through metrology and inspection data integration*
> **Authors**: Samsung Electronics × KLA Corporation (global leader in metrology and inspection equipment)
> **Published**: SPIE 2026, Metrology, Inspection, and Process Control XL, Vol. 13981
> **One‑Sentence Summary**: By fusing "metrology (quantified process parameters)" and "inspection (imaged defect results)" via machine learning, the system directs expensive inspection tools to scan only "the most defect‑prone areas," boosting the defect capture rate by roughly 3×, while also tracing defects back to their process root causes.


## 0. Preface: Why This Paper Is Worth Reading

If you are new to semiconductor yield‑related roles, you will inevitably deal with two core activities every day:

1. **Metrology**: Measuring various process parameters on wafers — line widths in nanometres, overlay alignment between layers, etc. It answers: **"How accurately was the process performed?"**
2. **Inspection**: Using optical or electron‑beam tools to find defects on wafers — shorts, opens, particles, bridges, etc. It answers: **"Where did it go wrong?"**

Traditionally, these two data streams are handled separately: metrology belongs to process engineers watching SPC charts; inspection belongs to yield engineers examining defect maps. The core idea of this paper is simple but powerful:

> **Where process parameters go abnormal is often exactly where defects cluster.** So why not use metrology data to "predict" where defects will occur, and then direct inspection tools to scan those areas first?

That is exactly what Samsung and KLA achieved with their **MDS (Multi‑Data‑Stream) platform**. The paper does not show off fancy model architectures; it uses the most conventional ML classifiers. Yet the engineering value is immense — because it addresses the real pain point in fabs: **e‑beam tools are too slow and too expensive for full‑wafer scanning, so sampling is inevitable — but where should you sample?**

### 1.2 Metrology Terms

|---|---|---|

| Term | Full Form / Original | Plain Explanation |

| SEM Review | Scanning‑electron‑microscope review | After brightfield finds thousands of candidates, a small subset is imaged by SEM to confirm whether they are true defects. |

| DOI | Defect of Interest | A genuine defect that actually affects yield. |

| Capture rate | — | The proportion of actual defects that are caught by your sampling scheme. This is the core metric in this paper. |
| Short defect | — | A short circuit between two lines that should not connect. The specific defect studied here is a line‑to‑contact short. |

### 1.4 Machine Learning Terms

| Term | Plain Explanation |
|---|---|
| Feature | Input variables fed to the model — here about 40 metrology parameters. |
| Label / Ground truth | The correct answer. In this paper, e‑beam full‑scan results serve as ground truth: whether a die has a defect or not. |
| Class imbalance | Defective dies are extremely rare while good dies are abundant; models tend to be lazy and predict "no defect" for everything. Special handling is required. |
| Class weighting | Making the model penalise "missing a defect" much more heavily than "falsely flagging a good die," forcing it to be sensitive to defects. |
| ROC / AUC | A curve and its area‑under‑the‑curve metric for classifier performance; the closer AUC is to 1, the better the discrimination. |
| Feature importance | The model tells you which input variables contribute most to its predictions — used here for root‑cause analysis. |
| Multicollinearity | When two features are highly correlated (e.g., top CD and bottom CD), keeping both can confuse the model; one can be dropped. |

## II. The Problem the Paper Addresses: The Pain of Sampling

### 2.1 The Real‑World Dilemma

- **Brightfield inspection (BF)** is fast and can scan every wafer, but it only reports suspicious points; a single wafer can produce **thousands of candidates**, of which **only a few percent are true defects (DOIs)**.
- **E‑beam** can reveal the truth, but it is too slow and too expensive. With hundreds of die per wafer, full scanning is unrealistic; you can only **sample**.

This leads to two fundamental questions — the paper states that the MDS platform was built precisely to answer these two fab‑level questions:

1. **"Where should we inspect or review next?"** 
2. **"How do these early findings relate to final yield outcomes?"**

### 2.2 Limitations of Traditional Approaches

- **Random sampling**: blindly picking die to scan. Sampling 19.4% of die typically catches only about 13.8% of defects — purely luck‑dependent.
- **Fixed‑rule sampling**: e.g., always scan wafer edges or fixed positions. This covers known issue patterns but is blind to new failure modes.

With limited resources, **"where to scan" is more valuable than "how to scan."** That is exactly MDS's entry point.


## III. MDS Platform Overall Architecture: Three Modules

MDS = **M**ulti **D**ata **S**tream, which consolidates multiple data streams (metrology, inspection, review) into an intelligent decision system. The three modules work together like an assembly line:

```
Metrology Data (CD/Profile/Overlay) ──┐
                                      ├─→ 【MGI】Predicts "where" defects exist → guides e‑beam sampling
Brightfield Inspection Candidates ────┤
                                      ├─→ 【MGR】Predicts which candidates are true defects → guides SEM review
E‑beam Review Ground Truth ──────────┘
                                      └─→ 【RCA】Feature importance analysis → tells process engineers "why"
```

The following diagram summarises the MDS platform's big picture (pain points → concept → three modules → future roadmap), giving you an overview before we dive into each module:

### 3.1 MGI: Metrology Guided Inspection

**What it does**: For every die on a wafer, it assigns a "defect probability score" and generates a **defect‑probability heatmap** — the darker the red, the higher the risk. E‑beam only scans the top N% of die with the highest scores.

**How it works**:
- Input: about **40 metrology features per die** (top/bottom CD, sidewall angle, recess depth, overlay, GOF, etc.);
- Labels: ground truth from e‑beam full‑scan (whether that die has a defect);
- Model: a binary classifier (the paper deliberately does not specify the exact algorithm; in practice, gradient‑boosted trees or logistic‑regression‑type models are common) that outputs "probability that this die has a defect."

**Intuition**: It is like weather forecasting. You do not need to knock on every door to know where it is raining — you look at air pressure, humidity, and cloud images to draw a precipitation probability map. Metrology features are "air pressure and humidity"; defects are "rain."

### 3.2 MGR: Metrology Guided Review

**What it does**: After brightfield inspection reports thousands of candidates, SEM review cannot look at all of them. MGR scores each candidate — "how likely it is a true DOI" — so that SEM only reviews the highest‑scored batch.

**Key innovation**: KLA already had a commercial algorithm, **SPOT®**, that uses only brightfield data (defect size, brightness, pattern attributes). MGR goes further by **feeding local metrology data at the candidate's location into the model** as well. The intuition: if an otherwise suspicious bright spot happens to sit in an area with large CD or overlay deviations, it is much more likely to be a real defect.

**One‑sentence result**: Signal‑to‑noise ratio improves dramatically; SEM no longer wastes time on false alarms.

### 3.3 RCA: Root Cause Analysis via Feature Importance

**What it does**: Model training is not the end. The model is interrogated: "Which features matter most to your decisions?" — the top‑ranked features reveal the process clues behind the defects.

This step transforms "black‑box prediction" into "white‑box insight," moving from **"catching defects"** to **"preventing defects."** See Section VI for a real‑world case study.


## IV. Data and Preprocessing: The Devil Is in the Details

Experimental data came from a real high‑volume **DRAM production line**, focusing on a specific layer that suffered from line‑to‑contact shorts.

### 4.1 Data Sources

| Data | Content | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Metrology data | About 40 features per die: CD, 3D profile parameters (sidewall angle, height, recess), multiple overlay values, plus secondary sensors (planarity, focus, tilt, etc.) | Model input X |
| E‑beam full‑scan | High‑resolution full‑scan of selected wafers, marking all true defect locations | Label Y for MGI (ground truth) |
| Brightfield inspection | About 1000 candidates per wafer with their attributes | One of MGR's inputs |
| SEM review | DOI / nuisance classification of candidates | Label Y for MGR |
| Electrical test | End‑of‑line electrical test data | Reserved for future yield prediction (not deeply explored in this paper) |

### 4.2 Four Preprocessing Steps (Highly Valuable for Engineering Practice)

1. **Feature reduction**: If the Pearson correlation between two features was too high, one was removed (e.g., when top CD and bottom CD were strongly correlated, only one was kept) to avoid multicollinearity.
2. **Normalisation**: All features were transformed to zero mean and unit variance to prevent large‑scale features (e.g., nanometre CDs) from dominating smaller‑scale ones (e.g., overlay errors).
3. **Class‑imbalance handling**: Defective die were extremely rare. MGI used **class weighting** (penalising missed defects far more than false alarms on good die); MGR used **oversampling of DOI samples + adjusted decision thresholds** to favour recall over precision.
4. **Wafer‑grouped data split**: This is a highly professional step — die on the same wafer share systematic similarities (same exposure, same etch). A random split would leak information between training and test sets, inflating metrics artificially. By splitting at the wafer level, the final model was validated on **completely unseen wafers (and even unseen lots)**, which is the real test of generalisation.


## V. Experimental Results: The Numbers Speak

> **Chart note**: All bar‑chart numbers in this section come directly from Table 1 and Section 4.2 of the paper; the capture‑rate curves, ROC curves, and process‑window charts are **schematics** drawn according to the paper's textual descriptions and figure captions (trends are real; exact shapes are illustrative), as noted in each figure title.

### 5.1 MGI: More Than 3× Capture Rate with the Same Budget

| Sampling Scheme | Scanning 19.4% of die | Scanning 35.1% of die |
|---|---|---|
| Random sampling capture rate | 13.8% | 28.5% |
| **MGI‑guided capture rate** | **62.9% (4.6×)** | **80.8% (2.8×)** |

![Comparison of defect capture rates: MGI vs. random sampling](../images/blog/20260810images/图1_MGI捕获率对比.png)

Looked at another way, the impact is even more striking: **MGI captures 49% of defects by scanning only 31% of die; to get 60% capture, MGI needs 40% scanning while random sampling would require over 60%**. For the fab, this means:

- More wafers can be processed with the same tool capacity (throughput gain);
- Or, with the same wafer volume, process excursions are detected earlier (problems are flagged earlier per lot).

Even more importantly, the generalisation performance: on wafers from **unseen lots (with process variations)** that were not used in training, MGI consistently outperformed the POR sampling scheme — proving that the model learned the underlying physical relationship between process parameters and defects, rather than merely memorising defect locations on a few wafers.

### 5.2 MGR: A Three‑Step Leap in Review Efficiency

With the same review budget of 31% of candidates:

| Scheme | DOI Detection Rate |
|---|---|
| Random sampling | ~31% (by definition) |
| SPOT® (brightfield data only, KLA's existing ML algorithm) | ~81% |
| **MGR (brightfield + metrology data)** | **~90%** |

![MGR three‑step leap and ROC curve schematic](../images/blog/20260810images/图2_MGR三级跳与ROC.png)

The ROC curve also shows the largest AUC for MGR — higher true‑positive rate and lower false‑positive rate. Note the critical detail: **the 81% → 90% gain came solely from adding metrology data**, providing the cleanest experimental proof of the value of "metrology × inspection" integration.

### 5.3 Official Conclusion in the Paper

The paper summarises: metrology guidance **increases defect capture rate by roughly 3× for a given sample size**, and guided review **catches nearly all true defects while reducing ineffective reviews by more than 20%**.


## VI. RCA Case Study: From "Catching Defects" to "Fixing the Process"

This is the most engineer‑flavoured part of the paper and deserves a detailed walk‑through.

### 6.1 The Model "Confessed" Two Top Suspect Features

Feature‑importance analysis on the MGI model ranked the top two features as:

1. **Bottom CD of the line**;
2. **Recess depth of the contact hole** (etch depth / remaining silicon height).

![Process window and feature importance schematic](../images/blog/20260810images/图3_工艺窗口与特征重要性.png)

### 6.2 Physical Interpretation Makes Perfect Sense

The defect under study is a **line‑to‑contact short**. For such a short to occur, the spacing between them must be too small:

- A wider **bottom** CD → more lateral space occupied → closer to the contact;
- A deeper etch of the contact (larger recess, thinner remaining silicon) → smaller vertical spacing.

The model is not learning mysticism; it is learning geometry. The paper also validated the data distribution: defective die indeed had systematically larger bottom CD and recess values, and **beyond a certain bottom‑CD threshold, the defect rate jumped abruptly** — directly delineating a **quantitative process‑window boundary**.

### 6.3 The Insight of Multivariate Synergy

The paper emphasises: **no single metrology reading perfectly separates good from bad die.** The true failure mode is a combination like "slightly larger overlay **plus** slightly larger CD" — each individually would pass a univariate SPC chart, but together they fail. This is the fundamental advantage of multivariate machine learning over traditional univariate process control: it captures **subtle interactions between features**.

Additionally, **GOF (Goodness of Fit) anomalies** from OCD also ranked high in importance — the aggregated signal that "the shape looks abnormal" is itself a leading indicator of defects, offering valuable guidance for designing monitoring rules.

### 6.4 Closed‑Loop Verification

Based on the RCA conclusions, the fab implemented corrective actions: **adjusted etch process to narrow bottom CD and tightened overlay control between critical layers**. Early data showed a clear decline in defect counts — the MDS insights were validated by real production. The loop from "detecting defects" to "preventing defects" was closed.


## VII. Critical Appraisal: Strengths and Gaps

### Strengths

1. **Problem‑driven, not model‑driven**. No fancy architectures; all design choices (class weighting, wafer‑grouped splits, feature reduction) serve production constraints — a textbook example of "machine learning deployed in industrial settings."
2. **Clean controlled experiments**. MGI vs. random sampling; MGR vs. SPOT® (only variable: inclusion of metrology data) — causal attribution is clear.
3. **Honest ground‑truth acquisition**. E‑beam full‑scan was used as ground truth, with the caveat that it only covers a subset of wafers.
4. **Emphasis on generalisation** (unseen lots/wafers), rather than just reporting training‑set metrics — avoiding the over‑optimism common in industrial AI papers.
5. **RCA closes the process loop**, with empirical evidence of "defect reduction after corrective actions" — not just armchair theorising.

### Gaps and Limitations (Reading Critically)

1. **Clickbait title**: The title includes "yield prediction," but the paper explicitly states that electrical test data are "left for future work"; the paper actually only covers **defect detection** — yield prediction remains a promise.
2. **Black‑box algorithm details**: No specific model, hyperparameters, or feature‑importance method (SHAP? Gini importance?) are disclosed — reproducibility is limited. This is understandable given it is a KLA commercial project (SPOT® is a product), but it remains a limitation.
3. **Single‑point validation**: Only one DRAM line and one defect type (line‑to‑contact short) were tested. The abstract mentions "cases across multiple layers and process steps," but the paper does not elaborate. Whether the approach works for logic layers, 3D NAND, etc., is unknown.
4. **Redacted key numbers**: Figure 5's process‑window threshold is labelled "~X nm" — business confidentiality, but it prevents readers from assessing the window width.
5. **Dependence on expensive ground truth**: Training MGI requires e‑beam full‑scan ground truth, which itself is costly to acquire; smaller fabs may not afford it.


## VIII. Key Takeaways for Yield Engineers

The ideas in this paper can be directly transferred to daily yield work:

1. **Data fusion > single‑point optimisation**. Metrology, inspection, review, and electrical test each have their own ceiling if modelled separately; feeding contextual data to the next step is the cheapest performance boost (MGR's 81% → 90% gain came solely from adding metrology).
2. **"Where to scan" is an optimisable decision problem**. Any monitoring system that relies on sampling (not just e‑beam) can ask: can I rank candidate locations using prior data?
3. **Feature importance is a free starting point for RCA**. Asking the model "what did you base that on?" after training is often more valuable than the model's predictions themselves — it directly points to process windows and corrective actions.
4. **Class imbalance and grouped splits are two major pitfalls in industrial defect data**; the paper's solutions (class weighting / oversampling + wafer‑grouped split) are directly actionable.
5. **Beware of single‑variable thinking**. Defects are often the result of "multiple parameters slightly out of nominal simultaneously"; a univariate SPC chart showing "in spec" is not a green light.
