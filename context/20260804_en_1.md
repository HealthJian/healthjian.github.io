# The Darkest Hour for "Wait-and-See" Builders: The Cycle, Causes, Trends, and Future of the All-Round PC Component Price Surge

## Executive Summary (TL;DR)

If you are planning to build a high-performance desktop PC, here is one piece of bad news and one piece of good news. **The bad news**: this is not an ordinary price hike, but the most violent super-cycle in the 40-year history of the memory industry—DRAM contract prices surged **93%–98%** quarter-on-quarter in Q1 2026, NAND flash rose another **70%–75%** in Q2, and the price increases have spread from memory and SSDs to graphics cards, hard disk drives, power supplies, coolers, and even cases and cables. A mid-range gaming PC in China has become **1,500–3,000 yuan more expensive** in just two months[^103^][^2^][^36^]. **The good news**: quarter-on-quarter price increases have visibly narrowed since Q3 2026 (DRAM slowed to +13%–18%), and the consumer spot market has already begun to ease and correct. The industry consensus is that **absolute prices will remain high until the second half of 2027, with the real downward inflection point most likely arriving in 2028**, when new capacity from Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron, and China's CXMT and YMTC comes online[^103^][^73^][^69^]. For "wait-and-see" builders: if you need it now, don't wait indefinitely—the steepest part of the surge is already behind us; if you don't need it urgently, you can wait, but the endpoint is H2 2027–2028, and it is almost impossible to return to the "bargain prices" of 2024.


## I. The Full Picture of Price Hikes: How Much Have Prices Actually Risen?

### 1.1 Record-Breaking Numbers

The core engine of this price surge is memory chips. According to TrendForce, conventional DRAM contract prices rose **93%–98%** quarter-on-quarter in Q1 2026—the largest single-quarter increase on record—followed by another **58%–63%** in Q2[^72^]. NAND flash surged in tandem: up 33%–38% in Q4 2025, 55%–60% in Q1 2026, and accelerating further to **70%–75%** in Q2—the first time in history that NAND's quarterly growth outpaced DRAM[^2^]. The spot market was even more extreme: Tom's Hardware's memory price index showed 16Gb DDR5 spot prices skyrocketing from about $6.84 in September 2025 to about $27.20 in December—a **298% increase in a single quarter**[^1^]. Kingston reported that NAND wafer costs had risen 246% by December 2025, with 70% of that increase concentrated in the last 60 days of the year[^2^].

![Quarter-on-quarter contract price growth for DRAM and NAND](../images/blog/assets/chart1.png)

The pass-through to retail has been equally shocking. In the Chinese market, a 32GB desktop DDR5 memory kit went from about 800 yuan in September 2025 to an average e-commerce price of about **3,800 yuan** in March 2026—a 375% increase in six months; 16GB DDR4 modules rose from about 200 yuan to about 890 yuan[^41^]. Graphics cards saw a second wave of jumps in late July 2026: the RTX 5060 rose from 2,300–2,500 yuan to 2,700–3,100 yuan, the RTX 5080 broke through 11,000 yuan, and Huaqiangbei channel prices changed "day by day"—morning quotes were obsolete by afternoon[^14^]. A 2TB SSD that cost just $110–130 during the 2024 promotional season had risen to $350–480 by mid-2026[^2^]; even the "sunset" hard disk drive saw wholesale prices rise for five consecutive quarters, with the Q2 2026 increase widening to 10%[^46^].

### 1.2 No Component Spared: Category Overview

Unlike 2017–2018 (mainly DRAM) or 2020–2021 (mainly GPUs), this round of price hikes covers almost every line item on a build list. The table below summarizes the magnitude, direct causes, and supply status for each category:

| Component Category | Increase Magnitude (mid-2025 → mid-2026) | Direct Cause | Supply Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| DRAM (DDR5) | Spot +298%/quarter; 32GB kit in China ~4×[^1^][^41^] | HBM wafer capacity cannibalization, fab reallocation | Allocated supply, servers prioritized |
| DRAM (DDR4/DDR3) | 16GB module in China ~+345%; Q3 contract prices forecast +50%[^41^][^93^] | Big 3 phase-out; enterprise SSD pulls demand back | Old models often more expensive than new |
| SSD (NAND) | Contract price ~4.2–4.5× over three quarters; 2TB retail ~3×[^2^] | Capacity prioritized for enterprise SSDs | Consumer segment "neglected" |
| Graphics Cards (GPU) | 16GB GDDR7 bulk +200%+; channel prices in China 20%–59% above MSRP[^13^][^27^] | VRAM cost pass-through + NVIDIA/AMD price adjustments | Some board partners suspend bulk shipments |
| HDD | ~+50% overall in six months; wholesale up 5 quarters straight[^38^][^46^] | AI data centers buying out annual capacity; no major expansion | WD/Seagate 2026 capacity sold out |
| Power Supplies / CPU Coolers | Warning hikes of 6%–10% / 6%–8%[^34^] | Rising raw material costs (copper, silver, etc.) | Promotions cancelled, new prices in effect |
| Motherboards / Cases / Cables | Widespread increases (builders say "each item went up")[^36^] | Rising costs of copper, aluminum, ABS plastics | Quotes updated daily |

At the system level, a builder at Zhengzhou's tech market told reporters that a mid-range gaming build that cost about 7,800 yuan two months ago now runs straight to **9,300 yuan**; high-end builds have risen 2,800–3,000 yuan in two months. Lenovo, ASUS, HP, and other branded desktops and gaming laptops have raised prices by 800–1,200 yuan[^36^]. Overseas markets are no different—IDC projects PC average selling prices will rise by up to 8% in 2026, and Sony, Nintendo, and Microsoft all raised console prices in 2026 for the first time ever, with Microsoft explicitly stating that "memory and storage costs have risen more than 2.5 times"[^1^].

### 1.3 A Footnote Often Overlooked: The "False Crash" of March 2026

Notably, this upward trend has not been a straight line. In March 2026, domestic memory prices experienced a **cliff-like plunge**: 16GB DDR5 single modules fell from a peak of 980 yuan to 680 yuan, and 32GB kits shrank from 3,800 yuan to 2,200 yuan—a drop of over 40%[^42^]. Three triggers converged: the mass production ramp of CXMT's second phase, improved DDR5 yields filling the consumer gap; industry-wide inventory recovering from 3–4 weeks to 6–7 weeks; and speculative hoarders who had treated memory as "electronic wealth management" facing margin pressure and panic selling[^42^].

But this correction proved to be only a "sentiment flush" in the spot market, not a trend reversal—upstream contract prices were still rising by 93%–98% that same quarter, and the two subsequently re-converged, with prices climbing again[^72^]. This episode offers an important lesson for wait-and-see builders: **retail spot prices are much more volatile than upstream contract prices**. Short-term plunges can happen at any time (especially during promotional events), but as long as contract prices are still rising, corrections are only "temporary buying opportunities," not "victory for the wait-and-see camp." The latest July 2026 data confirmed this again—DDR5 16GB spot prices fell back from a peak of 1,800 yuan to 1,000–1,200 yuan, while contract prices continued to rise over the same period[^88^].



## II. Component-by-Component Breakdown: Why Each Category Is Rising

### 2.1 DRAM: The Eye of the Storm

DRAM is the absolute protagonist of this price surge, and its logic chain can be summarized in one sentence: **AI data centers' demand for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) has sucked the life out of conventional DRAM capacity**. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron control more than 95% of global DRAM production. Producing 1GB of HBM consumes about 3–4 times the wafer area of conventional DDR5, while HBM margins are 2–3 times those of consumer DDR5—in a "wafer zero-sum game," every wafer allocated to NVIDIA's AI accelerators means one less consumer memory module[^1^][^3^]. TrendForce estimates that AI-related demand will consume about 20% of global DRAM wafer capacity in 2026; HBM's share of DRAM wafer output has risen from 19% in 2025 to 23% in 2026[^9^][^10^].

An even more symbolic event was the active retreat on the supply side: in December 2025, Micron announced its **exit from the consumer memory market**, shutting down its nearly 30-year-old Crucial brand to focus entirely on data center and HBM products[^10^]. Samsung and SK Hynix accelerated their phase-out of DDR4—resulting in a rare price inversion: DDR4 16Gb spot prices now routinely exceed those of comparable DDR5, and Q3 2026 DDR4 8Gb contract prices are forecast to rise another 50% quarter-on-quarter, driven by enterprise SSDs (each 1TB of NAND requires 1GB of dedicated DRAM cache) as they scale to 16–30TB and pull DDR4 demand back[^93^]. Even obsolete DDR3 has seen a demand revival, with Colorful reportedly considering re-releasing DDR3 motherboards—a vivid illustration of how severe the shortage has become[^45^].

### 2.2 Solid-State Drives (SSD): NAND's "Enterprise-First" Dilemma

The mechanism behind SSD price hikes is isomorphic to that of DRAM: AI servers consume several times more storage than traditional systems, and fabs prioritize NAND capacity for high-margin enterprise SSDs (eSSD), leaving consumer SSDs with only residual capacity[^2^][^102^]. TrendForce stated in its Q1 2026 report that quarterly orders "far exceeded supplier capacity," with client SSDs leading all NAND categories with over 40% quarter-on-quarter growth; Dell and Lenovo subsequently reduced default SSD capacities in their 2026 notebooks to control costs[^2^].

The ripple effects also spread to adjacent categories: NOR Flash and SLC NAND, which share capacity with mainstream NAND, saw contract prices cumulatively rise more than 100% in the first half of 2026[^2^]. After HDD shortages emerged, cloud vendors turned to high-capacity QLC SSDs to fill near-line storage gaps, further exacerbating NAND tightness—the various storage segments have formed a vicious cycle of "you're short, I'm short too"[^38^].

### 2.3 Graphics Cards: VRAM Cost Pass-Through + Vendor Pricing Power Devolution

The direct trigger for GPU price increases was VRAM. Bulk procurement prices for 16GB GDDR7 surged from $80–90 at the start of 2026 to $200–300—an increase of over 200%. For the RTX 5060 Ti 16GB, the VRAM alone added about 80% to the bill-of-materials cost[^13^]. In January 2026, NVIDIA notified partners of a 15%–25% across-the-board increase in GDDR6/GDDR7 procurement prices, and **cancelled the "GPU + VRAM" bundled sales model**, shifting the responsibility of sourcing VRAM to board partners—effectively passing the entire cost pressure to AIB brands. It then terminated its OPP open pricing plan subsidies, removing a "stabilizer" for end-user prices[^15^].

The second wave of price jumps in the Chinese market occurred around July 25, 2026: NVIDIA again raised GPU kit prices (GDDR7 rose nearly $20 per 2GB, adding about 560 yuan in VRAM cost for an 8GB card). Multiple graphics cards were temporarily removed from JD.com, and distribution prices from Colorful, MSI, and ASUS rose overnight by 600 yuan for 8GB models, 900 yuan for 12GB, and 1,200 yuan for 16GB[^11^][^27^]. AMD also implemented a roughly 10% price adjustment in July[^13^]. Compounding matters, NVIDIA proactively cut RTX 50-series gaming GPU production by 20%–30% (its data center business already accounts for 91.75% of revenue), and the RTX 50 SUPER series was delayed from early 2026 to Q3 due to 24Gb GDDR7 shortages[^15^][^25^]. Jensen Huang stated bluntly on the earnings call: "Gaming GPU supply will be very tight in the first half of 2026, and prices will significantly increase"[^15^].

### 2.4 Hard Disk Drives: A "Sunset" Industry Re-Ignited by AI

The HDD story carries a dramatic twist. The massive cold data generated by AI model training (images, videos, language archives) is extremely cost-sensitive on a per-GB basis, and HDD's unit cost is roughly 1/7 that of SSD—turning HDDs overnight from "bricks into gold bricks"[^55^]. On the supply side, the industry had been complacent: WD's CEO stated as early as October 2024 that "there is no need for expansion," and Seagate, with its 2026/27 capacity already sold out, still refuses to expand—the big three (Seagate, WD, Toshiba) prefer to constrain output to protect margins[^55^]. The result: both Western Digital and Seagate have confirmed that **essentially all of their 2026 HDD capacity has been pre-booked by AI data center customers**, with consumer-grade WD Blue 4TB rising from $70–80 to over $140[^38^].

The latest Q2 2026 data shows HDD bulk transaction prices rose 10% quarter-on-quarter, with 3.5-inch 1TB drives settling at about $58.9—the **fifth consecutive quarterly increase**, compared to previous quarterly increases of just 1%–4%[^46^]. There are also some unusual disturbances: a global helium supply crunch has reduced production of helium-filled HDDs (causing shortages for 16TB+ models), and Russia's helium export restrictions beginning in April 2026 have added cost pressure on upstream wafer fabs[^33^][^50^].

### 2.5 Other Components: The "Capillary" Pass-Through of Raw Material Inflation

If storage and GPUs are the "arteries," then the price increases for power supplies, coolers, motherboards, cases, and cables are the "capillaries"—driven by rising commodity prices for copper, silver, aluminum, plastics, and more. In January 2026, Guangzhou Xinhongzheng and other manufacturers sent warning letters to channel partners: power supplies would rise 6%–10%, CPU coolers 6%–8%, and all promotions would be cancelled from February onward[^34^]. Copper is widely used in motherboard PCBs, power supply windings, heat pipes, and graphics cards; aluminum is used for cases and heatsink fins; ABS/PC engineering plastics are used for housings and fan blades. These raw material cost increases, combined with storage costs, have created a comprehensive "each item went up" squeeze for system builders[^36^][^40^]. While the individual increases are modest (single digits to 10%), their cumulative effect has made the once-common budget target of "5,000–6,000 yuan for a mainstream gaming PC" nearly impossible to achieve in 2026[^34^].



## III. Root-Cause Analysis: Why All Components Surged at the Same Time

### 3.1 Surface-Level Cause: The AI Compute Arms Race

All threads ultimately lead to the same source—unprecedented capital expenditure on AI infrastructure. The world's 14 largest data center operators are approaching **$750 billion** in capex in 2026, and data centers are expected to consume about 70% of global memory chip production (compared to just 20%–30% in 2022)[^79^][^10^]. A single AI server uses roughly 8 times the memory of a traditional server, and AI server memory procurement is projected to expand from $35–40 billion in 2025 to $175–190 billion in 2027[^76^]. NVIDIA's Vera Rubin platform ramp-up and the continued pull of Agentic AI applications on x86 general-purpose server RDIMMs keep enterprise demand robust through the second half of 2026[^103^].

Crucially, the "payment structure" of this demand is different from the past. Hyperscale cloud providers (CSPs) are locking in capacity through **long-term supply agreements (LTAs)** —Micron's entire 2026 HBM capacity is already sold out; Kioxia is negotiating three-year LTAs through 2029; SanDisk extended its partnership with Kioxia to 2034; some customers have even proposed five-year contracts with Samsung through 2030[^10^][^45^][^81^]. Jefferies put it bluntly: no smartphone, PC, or console maker can sign such long-term agreements with memory suppliers—the consumer market is **structurally sidelined** in this capacity battle, forced to accept only residual capacity and price hikes[^73^].

### 3.2 Deep-Seated Causes: Oligopoly + Capacity Rigidity + Investment Vacuum

This shortage has been so intense because of three overlapping supply-side factors. **First, oligopoly structure**: more than 95% of global DRAM capacity is concentrated in Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron; HDDs are almost exclusively supplied by Seagate, WD, and Toshiba. Supply chains possess formidable "discipline"—the painful memory of industry-wide losses in 2023 (SK Hynix's full-year net margin was about -28%) has made manufacturers prefer shortages to reckless expansion[^1^][^59^]. **Second, capacity rigidity**: an advanced memory fab costs $15–20 billion and takes 2–3 years to build, creating a natural time lag between demand and supply response—this is the physical foundation of the memory industry's "cobweb cycle"[^66^][^62^].

**Third, and most overlooked: the 2024–2025 investment vacuum**. As IEEE Spectrum's memory experts noted, after memory prices fell below cost in 2023, Samsung took the rare step of cutting production by 50% to defend prices, and "all memory manufacturers have been extremely cautious about expansion ever since; through most of 2024 and 2025, there was virtually no new capacity investment"[^62^]. When AI demand exploded in 2025, the industry happened to be at a historical trough in capital expenditure—making the shortage unusually acute. Add in the "conversion loss" of HBM production lines (each transition to HBM consumes about 100,000–120,000 wafers per month of front-end capacity, equivalent to wiping out 300,000–350,000 wafers per month of conventional DRAM output), and the consumer market faces a double squeeze[^76^].

### 3.3 Amplifiers: Hoarding, Speculation, and "Daily Quotes"

On top of the structural shortage, channel behavior has amplified end-price volatility. During the second half of 2025, many merchants and outsiders treated memory modules as a "sure-win" investment, hoarding aggressively and artificially inflating prices; the March 2026 crash was precisely the result of panic selling of that speculative inventory[^42^]. In the July 2026 GPU price surge, channels switched to an "order-driven" model—collecting deposits to lock in prices before procuring—with Huaqiangbei distributors bluntly stating that "quotes are only valid for the day"[^14^][^52^]. CCTV Finance reporters in Huaqiangbei observed: a Kioxia 2TB SSD rose from 1,000 yuan to 1,500 yuan in a month; a KingBank 64GB memory kit rose from 3,000 yuan to 4,500 yuan; a roughly 12,500-yuan system became 25% more expensive in just one month simply due to storage price increases[^52^]. This dual-layer structure—"contract prices set the direction, spot prices amplify the volatility"—is the key framework for understanding the current market.

## IV. Historical Cycle Review: What Makes This Time Different

### 4.1 The Memory Industry's "Cobweb Cycle" Iron Law

Memory chips are among the most commoditized segments of semiconductors: standardized, interchangeable, with enormous fixed costs. For forty years, they have followed a 3–4 year "boom–bust" cycle—demand explodes, prices skyrocket → manufacturers reap windfall profits, massive expansion → 2–3 years later, new capacity comes online en masse → supply glut, price crash, industry-wide losses → production cuts, consolidation, wait for the next cycle[^66^][^68^]. A historical review using Micron as a sample shows: after the Windows PC super-cycle of 1993–1996, DRAM prices fell 51% in 1996 and another 65% in 1997; the 2016–2018 cloud + smartphone super-cycle pushed Micron's gross margin to a peak of 58.9%, followed by a DRAM price collapse of more than 50% in 2019; after the 2020–2021 pandemic cycle, 2022–2023 saw the "worst inventory glut in history," with NAND briefly falling below cash cost[^66^][^61^].

Cycle research has also distilled a set of actionable **leading indicators**: spot prices typically peak 3–6 months ahead of industry revenue peaks; inventory weeks exceeding 12–15 weeks are a warning sign (8–10 weeks at troughs); the time lag from capex announcements to capacity flood is about 12–18 months[^58^]. Applying this framework to the present: spot markets have softened since Q2 2026, Q3 contract price growth has narrowed to below 20%, fab inventories are at extremely low levels of 3–4 weeks but channel inventories are rising—"slope peaking" signals have appeared, but "absolute price peaking" has not yet arrived[^105^][^88^][^76^].

### 4.2 Comparison of Three Price Surge Cycles: 2017, 2021, 2025

Placing the current cycle in historical context reveals clear differences:

| Dimension | 2017–2018 DRAM Cycle | 2020–2021 GPU/Chip Shortage | 2025–2026 Storage Super-Cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core Driver | Smartphone capacity expansion + cloud + mining | Pandemic work-from-home + crypto mining | AI data centers + HBM capacity reallocation |
| Nature | Demand-pull | Demand-pull (with speculation) | **Supply-side active reallocation** |
| Peak Magnitude | DDR4 retail roughly doubled | GPUs long at 2–3× MSRP | DRAM contract +95% q/q; DDR5 spot +298% q/q[^72^][^1^] |
| Correction Path | Capacity caught up after 18 months, prices halved | Post-mining-crash + supply recovery, collapse | Institutions forecast high plateau until H2 2027–2028[^73^][^81^] |
| Post-Recovery Price | Returned to pre-surge levels | Largely returned to MSRP | Broad consensus: **will not return to 2024 lows**[^74^] |

The most essential difference between this cycle and the previous two is: **the shortage does not originate from consumers buying too much, but from supply being "siphoned off" by higher-margin products**. In 2017 and 2021, as long as demand receded (phone saturation, mining crash), prices would fall back quickly. But the 2026 logic is: as long as HBM margins remain 2–3 times those of consumer DDR5, fabs have no incentive to return wafers to the consumer market—this is largely independent of whether consumer demand retreats[^1^]. IDC calls this a "potentially permanent strategic reallocation of silicon wafer capacity"[^9^].

### 4.3 The "Cycle Is Broken" Debate: Is This Time Really Different?

The biggest debate in the industry today is whether memory's cyclical rhythm has been rewritten by AI. Evidence for the "broken" camp is substantial: the traditional four-year cycle should have peaked in 2025 and bottomed in 2027, but the growth rate peak arrived early in 2024 and has yet to confirm a top; shipments have far exceeded the previous two peaks. Hanwha Investment & Securities projects the 2026 global memory market at **$574.9 billion**—3.6 times the 2018 peak of $159.9 billion[^63^]. HBM directly determines the token output rate of large models, elevating memory from "compute accessory" to "core AI infrastructure" directly tied to productivity—an attribute absent from any previous cycle[^85^][^72^].

![Global memory market size cycles](../images/blog/assets/chart3.png)

But the "cycle will inevitably return" camp also has solid arguments. A former Samsung semiconductor executive publicly warned that **the AI-driven memory super-cycle will definitively end in the second half of 2027**; Michael Burry of "The Big Short" fame has publicly shorted Micron, with the simple reasoning that "the cyclicality of the memory industry is something no one can change"[^69^][^78^]. History always rhymes: in 2017–2018, manufacturers also touted that "data center demand will end cycles," only to see capacity oversupply of 20%–30% and prices collapse 60%–70% in 2019[^58^]. The three major fabs are currently pouring record cash flows into expansion (SK Hynix's Korean investment exceeds $100 billion; Micron's long-term US investment raised to $250 billion)—and these capacities are precisely scheduled to ramp up in 2027–2028, following the same script as previous cycles[^78^]. An even more subtle signal: memory stocks entered a bear market in mid-2026 "against the strongest fundamentals in history," and historically, stock prices always peak 1–2 quarters ahead of fundamentals[^59^][^66^].

## V. Trends and the Future: Where Is the Inflection Point?

### 5.1 Second Half of 2026: Growth Narrowing, But Not Price Drops

As of early August 2026, the most certain judgment is: **prices are still rising, but the pace has slowed noticeably**. TrendForce's early July forecast projected Q3 DRAM contract prices up 13%–18% quarter-on-quarter and NAND up 10%–15%—the first time in over a year that quarterly growth has fallen below the 20% threshold[^103^]. PC DRAM in Q4 is estimated to narrow further to +3%–8%[^89^]. The narrowing is not due to improved supply, but because **consumer tolerance has reached its limit**: PC OEMs have high inventory and little willingness to accept another round of hikes; smartphone makers who raised prices to offset LPDRAM costs have in turn suppressed handset sales[^103^][^105^]. In other words, the market is approaching the "pain threshold," and the marginal space for further price increases is capped by demand contraction.

However, some segments are still accelerating and must be treated separately. In the enterprise market, with CSPs locked into LTAs, pricing pressure shifts to customers without LTAs; Adata chairman Chen Li-pai said Q3 DRAM contract prices could rise another 20%–30% and NAND 35%–40%, with "NAND's upward momentum clearly stronger than DRAM in the second half"[^98^][^83^]. Older DRAM like DDR4/DDR3, due to the big three's phase-out and enterprise SSD pull, could see Q3 contract prices rise another 50% q/q, with supply gaps possibly lasting two years[^93^]. For GPUs, the late-July price adjustments have just landed, and channels expect more VRAM increases ahead, with some distributors already hoarding cards in anticipation of further rises[^27^]. Consumer spot markets, by contrast, are easing due to "can't buy anymore"—DDR5 16GB spot has fallen from a peak of 1,800 yuan to 1,000–1,200 yuan, offering a temporary window for urgent buyers[^88^].

### 5.2 2027: A High Plateau with Internal Divergence

Mainstream institutions are highly aligned on 2027: **prices will remain high, but different segments will begin to diverge**. For DRAM, HBM's share of DRAM wafers will rise to 31%, and server RDIMM supply (by capacity) will grow only 15%–20% in 2027—far below server CPU shipment growth—so TrendForce expects server DRAM contract prices to rise quarter-by-quarter through H2 2027 (with progressively narrowing increments)[^88^][^101^]. Xiaomi president Lu Weibing and Honor executive Lin Lin have both publicly judged this storage price upcycle as a long one, with prices remaining high through the end of 2027 or even 2028[^81^].

SSDs may see an earlier inflection: with YMTC Phase III production ramping (monthly capacity climbing from 200,000 wafers toward 300,000–500,000) and new NAND capacity coming online, consumer SSD supply-demand could turn looser and prices fall earlier in H2 2027[^88^][^83^]. UBS has pushed its NAND cycle peak from Q2 2027 to Q3, precisely due to YMTC Phase III capacity allocation plans—a single Chinese manufacturer's production schedule can now sway global institutional price forecasts[^83^]. For HDDs, analysts expect supply-demand relief only in 2027, but by then AI storage demand will likely grow enough to absorb new capacity, so expectations should remain modest[^38^].

### 5.3 2028: Capacity Flood and Cycle Reversal Risk

2028 is the convergence point for nearly all forecasts. Jefferies expects truly significant price moderation only in 2028—when new wafer capacity gradually comes online and Chinese manufacturers join in, global supply could increase 15%–20%, and if AI demand simultaneously slows, average selling prices will then fall[^73^]. Intel and controller maker Silicon Motion also point to 2028 for "normalization"[^1^]. The supply-side timeline supports this judgment:

![Timeline of new memory capacity additions](../images/blog/assets/chart4.png)

Samsung's Pyeongtaek P5 will only ramp up in mid-2028 and reach full capacity in 2029; SK Hynix's Yongin cluster will be completed in Q2 2027; Micron's Idaho plant will start production in H2 2027, and its New York flagship fab in mid-2028; on the domestic front, CXMT's new Shanghai fab, with a planned monthly capacity of 400,000–600,000 wafers, will begin mass production in 2027, targeting total capacity of 1 million wafers/month within three years (5× the 2025 level); YMTC Phase III will raise NAND monthly capacity to 300,000–500,000 wafers, enough to meet about 15% of global 3D NAND demand[^80^][^69^][^83^]. After the three major fabs' new wave of DRAM capacity lands in 2027–2028, global annual DRAM capacity could exceed 30 million wafers, and the market will tip into outright oversupply[^69^].

Historical experience suggests that capacity floods combined with marginal demand slowdown often lead to **overcorrection**: what arrives in 2028 may not be a "moderate easing" but a new downward cycle. Multiple institutions have already warned that memory company earnings in 2027–2028 will face consecutive declines due to high bases and falling prices—and those cloud vendors who signed five-year LTAs at peak prices may find themselves locked into the highest procurement prices in history[^69^][^78^]. For consumers, this means 2028 is likely the best system-building window in years—even if prices don't return to 2024 levels.

### 5.4 The Biggest Variable: Sustainability of AI Capital Expenditure

All 2027–2028 forecasts rest on one premise: that AI capex does not reverse. This premise is not unassailable—in 2025, the top five hyperscalers' capex-to-revenue ratio exceeded 22%, compared to a historical four-year average of just 11%–16%; the pressure for mean reversion is real. If it reverts to the historical average, 2026 capex could theoretically face a 20%–30% correction[^77^]. Caixin's economist checklist is worth bookmarking: **global semiconductor sales y/y growth, DRAM export prices y/y, quarterly memory contract price growth, and cloud capex guidance**—the price growth slope likely peaks in H2 to Q4 2026[^85^].

If AI capex growth slows, memory prices could **peak and fall earlier**. Conversely, if inference demand (especially Agentic AI's KV cache storage requirements, which scale linearly with user count and conversation length) continues to exceed expectations, the high-price plateau could extend beyond 2028[^69^][^76^]. Another structural variable is the pace at which Chinese capacity comes online: SemiAnalysis expects CXMT's share of global DRAM supply to rise from about 11% in 2025 to about 17% in 2028, while Jefferies cautions that the short-term (2026–2027) impact of Chinese capacity will be concentrated on low-end and mature domestic products—the catch-up in high-end server DRAM and HBM will still take time[^73^].

## VI. Practical Guide for Wait-and-See Builders: Build Now, or Keep Waiting?

### 6.1 First, Answer the Soul-Searching Question: What Can Waiting Actually Bring?

Based on the evidence chain in Section V, three levels of judgment can be offered. **First, you won't get a "crash"**: this is a supply-reallocation shortage; as long as HBM's margin advantage persists, fabs' capacity is sold out, and LTAs lock up supply, there is no 2021-style crash mechanism. The March 2026 spot plunge was already "the worst moment's rebound," not a trend reversal[^42^]. **Second, you can get "growth narrowing + spot corrections"**: from Q3 onward, contract price growth has narrowed to below 20%, and consumer spot markets are experiencing periodic softening under demand contraction—Double 11, 618, and other promotional events will still offer relative lows[^105^][^88^]. **Third, the real "victory" lies in H2 2027–2028**: SSDs may see price drops first in H2 2027, while DRAM and overall platforms will only see significant declines after new capacity arrives in 2028—but the broad industry expectation is that we will not return to the "bargain prices" of 2024[^88^][^74^].

Translating "waiting" into a financial calculation is clearer: a mid-range build today costs about 2,000–4,000 yuan more than its low point in 2025. If you skip buying now and wait until 2028, you might save most of that premium—but the cost is not having a new PC for two years. **A computer is a productivity tool and entertainment device, not a financial asset.** The opportunity cost of waiting is exactly those two years of usage value. Unless your current machine is fully sufficient, "holding out to wait for a price drop" is not cost-effective for most scenarios.

### 6.2 Decision Matrix by Urgency Level

| User Type | Recommendation | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Urgent (old machine dead / work critical) | **Buy now, but change how you buy**: prioritize memory and SSD, order during spot correction windows; buy only what you need now, not "future-proof" capacity | Steepest part of the surge is over but direction remains upward; spot correction windows (e.g., DDR5 16GB from 1,800 to ~1,000 yuan) are the best entry points in nearly a year[^88^] |
| Semi-urgent (current machine usable but poor experience) | **Upgrade partially, not whole system**: add memory or SSD for the biggest boost; keep old GPU or buy used for now | Partial upgrades minimize exposure to price hikes; GPUs with high premiums are better to wait for a generation refresh |
| Wait-and-see (no rush at all) | **Set trigger conditions before buying**: ① contract prices turn negative for two consecutive quarters; ② cloud capex guidance revises downward; ③ from H2 2027, watch for SSD as the first segment to fall | The three signals correspond to cycle inflection, demand inflection, and segment inflection[^85^][^88^] |
| Chasing new (waiting for RTX 50 SUPER / next gen) | Lower expectations: SUPER series already delayed due to GDDR7 shortages, VRAM costs up 100%+, launch prices will likely be "far higher than previously expected"[^25^][^24^] | New products plus VRAM inflation—"more for the same price" is unlikely in this cycle |

### 6.3 Component-by-Component Buying Strategy

**DRAM and SSD: buy for now, not for the future.** These are the two categories most central to contract price increases and also the most volatile in spot markets. The strategy is to buy your **current required capacity** in one go during spot correction windows (promotional events, sentiment-driven drops like March or July), but do not stockpile modules for upgrades two years down the line—after 2028 capacity arrives, the high-priced memory you hoard today will likely depreciate. DDR4 platform users should act decisively: the big three are continuing to phase out DDR4, Q3 contract prices are still up 50%, and older models will only become more expensive[^93^]. Domestic memory chips (CXMT DDR5, YMTC NAND modules) offer relatively lower-priced alternatives in this cycle, and their capacity is ramping up fastest—they are worth prioritizing[^42^][^83^].

**Graphics cards: differentiate by model, watch 8GB and used markets.** The magnitude of price increases is positively correlated with VRAM size—8GB models (RTX 5060, 5060 Ti 8GB) have seen the smallest increases and in some channels even price inversions; 16GB+ models carry the heaviest premiums (40%–59% above MSRP)[^13^][^27^]. Budget-conscious gamers should seriously consider 8GB mainstream models or last-gen inventory cards; although the used market has also followed the uptrend (RTX 3060 12GB up 100–200 yuan), its premium rate is still lower than new cards[^14^]. If you don't chase ray-tracing frame rates, an iGPU / old-card transition plus a GPU upgrade in 2028 is a completely rational path.

**Hard disk drives: don't wait if you need one.** Seagate and WD's 2026 capacity is sold out and they have explicitly ruled out expansion; waiting will not bring price drops, but may instead lead to stockouts. High-capacity NAS drives (14TB+) have seen the largest increases, while 4–8TB consumer drives are relatively milder (+20%–35%)[^38^][^39^]. **Power supplies, coolers, and case/PSU/cooling**: individual increases are 6%–10%—no need to stockpile; buy them together with the whole system, but accept the new normal of "promotions cancelled, new prices in effect"[^34^].

### 6.4 A Signal Checklist for Wait-and-See Builders

Distilling complex industry analysis into actionable moves, just watch four types of public signals. **Signal 1: TrendForce quarterly contract prices**—when DRAM/NAND contract price growth narrows to single digits for two consecutive quarters or turns negative, the cycle top is confirmed; we are currently in the first stage of "growth narrowing"[^103^]. **Signal 2: Cloud capex guidance**—if Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta all revise their capex guidance downward in earnings reports, memory demand expectations will be immediately revalued, and prices may peak early[^85^][^77^]. **Signal 3: Chinese capacity ramp-up**—the production progress of CXMT's Shanghai fab in 2027 and YMTC Phase III capacity utilization—these are the most certain supply-side increments[^69^][^83^]. **Signal 4: Channel inventory and spot/contract price spread**—when spot prices persistently fall below contract prices and channel inventory weeks return to above 6–8 weeks, the downward inflection is near (historically, inventory above 12–15 weeks is a danger zone)[^58^][^42^].

Finally, a piece of psychological advice: the wait-and-see builder's opponent is never "profiteers," but the fab construction timelines. In this cycle, the rational goal of "waiting" is not to return to the past, but to **avoid the steepest surge, buy during the narrowing and correction windows**—that window has already opened in H2 2026. Or simply lock your budget, accept the new philosophy of "buy what you need, buy enough, prioritize domestic options," and save the premium for the likely overcapacity-driven price drop cycle of 2028.
